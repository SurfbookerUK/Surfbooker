Choosing a Garden Designer

It is not easy to find the right garden designer, because many courses and diplomas allow people to use the title. A good designer needs wide knowledge: garden and architectural history, horticulture, climate and geography, topography and geology, art and garden styles, safety, construction and legal restrictions. Formal training matters, but hands-on experience matters more.

The article argues that a local designer is generally more dependable than one working from another country, because local plants, soils and weather vary so much.

Garden design is a staged process. Its core task is organising space into outdoor rooms for recreation, dining or play, which, being made of plants, change with the seasons.

The designer should visit the site and meet you in person. The first meeting should cover:

  • Your brief and the place: how you want to use the garden, children or pets, and the style and colours of the house.
  • The space: entrances, boundaries, neighbours and features such as large trees, water points and windows, ideally in a rough sketch.
  • What lies beyond the garden, which helps decide what to hide or reveal, plus orientation, so you know where sun and shade fall, and topography: a sunken garden collects more water.
  • Wind, which can limit use and may call for trees chosen as windbreaks.
  • Borders and fences: height and material rules, and whether a fence should open a view or give security.
  • Drainage and irrigation, what exists and what is needed, and whether plant choice can manage water instead.
  • Slopes, which should be mapped and can be used to add interest.
  • Utilities: water, gas, electricity and sewage, which need access and should not lie under hard surfaces or large shrubs.
  • Soil: structure and pH decide what will thrive, and existing plants offer clues. Camellias struggle in heavy clay, for example, viburnums prefer alkaline soil, and conifers acidify soil, which suits rhododendrons but also encourages horsetail.
